# For the Draft

The Spool Street lift went down on payday, which was good luck twice over: the
Concord pays same-day rates for a fast splice, and the Brass Lantern sits at the
bottom of Spool Street.

I had the cable trued a little after dark — though dark is a manner of speaking.
There's no sky in the Undercroft. Dusk is just when the lamps come on, and the
lamps come on when Tovin gets to them, one ladder at a time, in an order he has
kept longer than I've been splicing. You can tell the hour by which corner of the
district is still waiting on him.

The Lantern was half full. Dagan was in his spot by the wall, coughing his
set-house cough between sips, and two Waterline men were arguing about the fish
quota in dock-cant, the way you argue about a thing that's been decided somewhere
above your head. The fish plate cost two coppers more than it had in Thaw. Nobody
said anything about it. What is there to say.

Wick had my message-glass waiting behind the bar, wrapped in a rag like a
sandwich.

"It was the seat pin," he said. "Also, you dropped it."

"It fell."

"It fell out of your hand, from a height." He set it in front of me and slid my
cup after it. "If it falls again, buy a new one. From someone else."

That's Wick being fond. You learn to hear it.

The appraiser was at the end of the bar again. He'd been coming in maybe two
weeks by then — a quiet fellow, soft voice, a dealer in old devotional pieces.
He'd bought a votive lamp off Sella's boy earlier that week, the little grey one
their family kept over the door, and paid better than it was worth, which people
appreciated. He remembered names. He asked after Dagan's cough once and meant it,
as far as I could tell. I never saw him drunk and I never saw him hurry.

That night he had a piece of embroidery spread on the bar, some feast-day banner
gone brown at the folds, and he was going over it the way I go over a frayed
cable, which I suppose is his job.

"You get much of this kind of thing down here?" he asked Wick. Friendly. "Old
walls like these. People must find pieces when they knock through."

"They find rats," Wick said. "Occasionally a pipe nobody remembers laying."

"No little saints in the brickwork?"

"If there were, the rent would go up."

The appraiser smiled and let it go, and his eyes went along the shelf behind the
bar and stopped on the candle, the way everyone's do eventually. Wick keeps
setwork lamps in every corner — good ones, he tunes them himself — and then that
one true flame on the bar in a brass dish, tallow, lit every hour I've ever been
in the place.

"That's a real flame," the appraiser said. "You don't see many anymore."

"It's for the draft," Wick said.

The man glanced around the room, which sits under forty feet of city and has
never felt a draft in my lifetime or anyone's. "Of course," he said, pleasantly,
and that was that. It's an old joke. The regulars have each made it once and then
stopped making it. You get the sense that's how Wick prefers it.

Tovin came in near the end of his rounds with the long ladder on his shoulder and
soot on his cuffs, and Wick had the kettle going before the door shut. Tovin
doesn't take ale on rounds nights. He drinks hot water with a spoon of honey in
it, like a man being gently punished.

The appraiser paid for the honey. "For the lamplighter," he said. "Best-kept
lamps in the district. I've been admiring them."

"Oh," Tovin said. "Thank you. They're Concord lamps. I only keep them."

"You'd know the district better than anyone, though. The old routes. I've been
trying to learn it — my trade, you understand; old pieces turn up where the old
buildings are. Are any of the shrines still, you know." He turned his cup a
little. "Kept? Flowers, that kind of thing."

It wasn't a strange question, from a man in his line. I want to say that plainly,
because of what I noticed next, which is that Tovin went still. Not upset. Still,
the way old men go still when you ask them about the war they were in — not
refusing the question, just gone somewhere to check on something before
answering.

"There's the tide-day garlands, down the Waterline," he said at last. "People
keep the customs more than the places."

"The customs more than the places," the appraiser said. "That's well put." And he
wrote nothing down and asked nothing else, and bought Dagan's next cup on his way
out, and said he'd be around until the month turned, if anyone came across old
pieces they'd rather turn into money.

The door swung shut. Wick watched it longer than a door needs watching.

"Nice enough fellow," I said.

"Mm," Wick said. It's what he says instead of arguing.

Tovin finished his honey water. He looked tired, but he'd looked tired since
Highsun; his rounds don't get shorter and he won't take on a boy to help. He
stood, shouldered the ladder, and paused at the bar beside the candle, and
trimmed its wick with his fingers, offhand, the way you'd fix a shirt tag on
someone you know. Then he apologized to nobody in particular, the way he does,
and went out to finish the lamps.

I walked home up Spool Street under my own good splice, all the lamps steady,
all of them humming their one note. Fifty-one lamps between the Lantern and my
stair; I've counted. They never gutter and they never go out, and if one did, it
would be my problem by morning, at same-day rates.

It's a fine thing, a street you can see on. My mother still says a candle-end
cost an hour's wages in her mother's day. I thought about that, and about the
fish plate, and about nothing much else, and slept fine.
